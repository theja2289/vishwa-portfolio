Gelly: autonomous micro-assembly under vision Picks microscopic spring-probe gangs from wafer trays, aligns them on a glass slide under micron vision, breaks the tethers, loads the finished slides. In production
ProblemParts measured in microns, handled by people under a microscope, at a rate and consistency that could not scale. Every unit of yield loss was invisible until much later.
ApproachA closed loop across vision, ACS motion and behaviour trees, with the decision layer owning when to retry, when to compensate and when to stop. Error compensation holds placement across a whole fleet of machines rather than one golden unit.
Result±1 µm placement held across the fleet. Station cycle time from 15 s to under 5 s. In-line verification from 300 ms to 50 ms.

One framework, a hundred cells A universal automation framework where a new cell is configured and simulated rather than written from scratch. In production
ProblemEvery new automation cell was a bespoke build. Nothing carried over, so each one cost years and the knowledge lived in whoever wrote it.
ApproachBuilt the framework on RoboDK and behaviour trees, covering simulation, environment, CI/CD, fleet management and observability. Around 130 reusable skills and 160+ behaviour trees, with dry-run preview and resource footprints that block conflicting parallel motion. An in-browser editor lets an engineer compose behaviour instead of writing code.
ResultCell bring-up from roughly three years to three months, about three engineers a year of effort avoided. UR3, UR5, Meca500, Epson SCARA and custom platforms all run on it.

GellyML: replacing a commercial vision stack with a learned one Swapped Cognex VisionPro for an in-house pipeline, rolled out in shadow mode alongside production before anyone switched over. In production
ProblemA hand-tuned commercial vision stack, licensed per station, brittle against anything it had not seen, and impossible to version or review like code.
ApproachBuilt GellyML in Python, OpenCV and PyTorch: a browser vision editor, versioned text-based jobs with publish and rollback, YOLOv8 detection and PatchCore anomaly detection. Ran it in shadow mode against production for long enough to compare it honestly before cutting over.
ResultInspection now catches defect classes that previously only an operator caught. Vision jobs are reviewable, diffable and reversible.
PyTorchOpenCVYOLOv8 PatchCoreONNX RuntimePython
Enhanced Traceability, fab to assembly Part identity that survives the step which normally destroys it, carried all the way to final assembly. In production
ProblemIdentity died at singulation. One wafer becomes many parts, and from that point the box label was the only record of where anything came from. Downstream, people retyped numbers into a system by hand.
ApproachDesigned and delivered the whole spine. Lot ID laser-scribed on the wafer back side at incoming, a centre mark placed before singulation so every piece carries its parent, direct marking on gang bars, probe heads and guide plates, and tablet QR scanning at each station writing to MES in real time. Also wrote the rule for what gets marked at all: only items whose interchange carries a quality or process risk, so laser time is not spent on fasteners.
ResultIdentity survives singulation and manual entry is gone from process stations. First author, FormFactor Technology Summit 2025.
laser markingMES integrationQR / direct part marking robotic transferdata model design
Software that does not take the line down A monolithic desktop application re-architected into a headless engine with isolated workers, shipped into an air-gapped factory on a weekly cadence. In production
ProblemOne WPF desktop application held motion, vision and the state machine in a single process. Any fault anywhere stopped the tool, and releasing anything into a running factory was a risk nobody wanted to take often.
ApproachRe-architected to a headless .NET 9 engine with separate motion and vision worker processes and ZeroMQ messaging between them, with the process boundaries and the state model defined up front. Behind it: around 1,700 automated tests, an 83-check engine self-test, Playwright browser checks, and GitLab CI/CD with build, validate, test, per-tool config preview and manual-gated production deploys.
ResultFive production releases in ten weeks into an air-gapped factory. A crash in one worker no longer stops the tool. Around 450k lines on the platform branch.

MotionVitals AI: clinical movement assessment on the device Pose estimation moved off the cloud and onto an NPU, built in 24 hours. First place, Compute Track, ExecuTorch × Qualcomm 2026. Shipped and judged
ProblemMovement assessment that depends on a round trip to a server is not usable at the point of care, and it puts patient video somewhere it should not be.
ApproachAs system architect and lead engineer on a team of five, lowered RTMPose onto the Qualcomm Hexagon NPU through ExecuTorch and wrote the native C++ runtime streaming tensors behind it, proving native ARM64 ahead-of-time export on Windows on ARM.
ResultPose inference from 89 ms to 1.6 ms, a 55× speedup, running live at 30 fps on device. 223 tests and CodeQL in CI, in 24 hours. First place in the Compute Track.

Force-controlled insertion with no direct feedback A ROS 2 policy inserting fibre-optic connectors on a UR5, built with a global team of five. 36th of 1,600 teams, Intrinsic AI for Industry Challenge 2026. Prototype
ProblemInsert SC and SFP connectors autonomously when the port gives no direct feedback about whether you are aligned, and the tolerance is smaller than the uncertainty in where the port is.
ApproachLed the team and set the architecture, splitting the work across vision, fiducial, geometric-estimation and learned-policy tracks so they could be compared rather than argued about. Designed the layered recovery: compliant approach, force bias, spiral search.
ResultPolicy runs end to end on a UR5 under force/torque control, on both SC and SFP ports. 36th of 1,600 teams, the top 2.3%.

Piezo fatigue detethering workcell A characterization rig that separates parts by driving them to fatigue, rather than cutting them. Prototype
ProblemTethers holding microscopic parts to a gang bar have to be broken without damaging anything nearby, and the gang bar sags when probes are missing, which changes the mechanics every time.
ApproachDesigning the workcell around a Meca500 arm, a Thorlabs NanoMax piezo stage and telecentric vision, with an electromagnetic blade to hold the bar flat: a soft iron bar with about 50 turns of 30 AWG wire, MOSFET switched, around 50 mA at 12 V. Corrected the gang bar mass to 12 mg, which put the inertial force at 5 kHz and 10 µm travel at roughly 1.2 mN and set the hold current.
ResultTwo-tier BOM scoped, from a bench proof around $17k to a full cell around $90k. Currently a bench effort, not a production system.
piezo stagesThorlabs NanoMaxtelecentric vision electromagneticsMEMS handlingfixture design
Large-area submicron imaging architecture Getting submicron detail over a large field without paying for the optics that would normally be required. Design study
ProblemResolution and field of view trade against each other. Covering a large area at submicron resolution either means a very expensive objective and a long tiling scan, or giving up one of the two.
ApproachArchitected a hybrid: a CIS sensor capturing the large field, with a 4× super-resolution model recovering detail, so the optical budget buys field of view and the model buys resolution. Alongside it, evaluated the camera path end to end, comparing Teledyne FLIR Blackfly S and Basler dart and ace 2 against the incumbent endoscope, and specified custom Gorilla Glass slides with sharp 90° edges, including which manufacturing order the ion exchange has to come in.
ResultAn architecture and a costed component path. Not built, and I would want to prove the super-resolution step against ground truth before anyone relies on it for metrology.
CIS sensorssuper-resolutionFLIR / Basler optical designsubstrate sourcing
A network instruments can live on Segmented OT network and browser-based remote access for automation tools that cannot be exposed to a corporate LAN. In production

ApproachDesigned a four-VLAN architecture separating operational technology, build, DMZ and corporate traffic, and put Apache Guacamole in front of it as a browser-based access gateway, so support happens through one audited door instead of a VPN into the tool network. Run two self-hosted GitLab instances and the end-to-end CI/CD behind it, serving around 60 engineers.
ResultTools reachable without being exposed. Also completed a Git LFS migration that cut repository size by 99.6%.

SmartBox: instrumented storage for fragile parts Storage that knows what is inside it, what condition it is in, and reports without being asked. Design study
ProblemFragile, expensive assemblies sit in storage between process steps with no record of handling, environment or damage, so a problem only appears when something fails much later.
ApproachDistributed micro-sensors in the enclosure, self-calibrating 3D inspection, and secure telemetry out. Worked up as a 28-claim provisional patent draft with a full bill of materials, an ROI model and an internal pitch.
ResultA complete concept with the economics worked out. The provisional is drafted and not filed.

Working together

Three ways this usually starts

Walk the process
2 to 3 weeks

I come in, follow the workflow end to end, and instrument where the time and the errors actually go. You get a written account of what is worth automating, what is not, and what it would cost. Often the answer is smaller than people expect.

Build the thing
scoped per project

Design and deliver the cell, the pipeline or the data spine, with the tests, the documentation and the handover so your own people can run it without me. I would rather leave something maintainable than stay indispensable.

Embedded lead
ongoing, part time

Technical ownership of an automation effort you are already running: architecture, reviews, hiring input and unblocking, alongside whoever is doing the day-to-day build.
